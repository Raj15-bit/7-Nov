The big picture: money ran from the AI crash into India

US chip shares had their worst 5 days since 2001, and foreign funds moved part of that money into Indian shares.

In short
  • US and Asian technology shares fell hard as the AI trade broke.
  • Foreign funds bought Indian shares, and the Nifty had its best run before Diwali since 1990.
  • Indian funds sold into that rally, and most smaller shares did not join it.

2 markets moved in opposite directions. Shares linked to artificial intelligence (AI) crashed in New York, Seoul and Taipei. Indian large companies, which have little AI exposure, rose sharply as foreign funds looked for a market that does not depend on the AI story.

Why the money moved

The AI selloff was driven partly by rules, not news. Funds that must cut risk when prices swing sold automatically. US election results added to it, because new state leaders want to pause data centre building. India gained from both, because its big companies are banks, carmakers and consumer firms.

What the rally hides

One view is that India has become the world's "anti-AI trade", a place to hide when technology shares fall. The opposite view is that this money is fast and can leave as quickly as it came, since much of it was foreign funds closing bets on a fall rather than buying for the long term.

Reading between the lines, the same event that helped India also carries risk for it. If the AI bust deepens into a US credit problem, global funds may sell everything, India included. If AI shares recover, the money may go back. Section 12 sets out both sides.

The AI trade cracks in the US and Asia

Chip shares fell at a pace not seen since 2001, forced selling did much of the damage, and data centre plans met new limits.

In short
  • US chip shares lost more than 20% of their value in 5 sessions.
  • Funds that run on fixed rules were forced to sell, which made the fall faster.
  • New state leaders in the US want to pause data centres, which hurts AI spending plans.

The size of the fall

The Philadelphia chip index (SOX) fell more in 5 sessions than at any time since September 2001. On 5 November alone it dropped 12.3%. People who borrowed to bet on chips were hit hardest.

Much of the money had arrived only weeks earlier. US leveraged funds (funds that use borrowed money to double or triple a move) took in a record $9.5bn in a single October week, and $7bn of that went into the triple chip fund.

Selling by rule, not by choice

Nomura's Charlie McElligott counted the forced selling. Leveraged funds must rebalance at the close, and "volatility control" funds cut shares when prices swing. Together they sold about $165bn. Thursday 5 November was also the biggest day for buying put options (insurance against a fall) in Cboe history.

Views split on what this means. One view is that the crash came from plumbing: borrowed money was unwound, and hedge funds did not turn bearish on AI. The opposite view points to record US margin debt (money borrowed to buy shares), about 50% above past peaks against the size of the economy, and says more forced selling can follow.

Data centres meet the voters

The 3 November elections changed who runs several states. Candidates who promised to pause data centre building now govern states that hold 41% of the planned US pipeline. In Texas, Gina Hinojosa beat Greg Abbott, and a poll found Texans oppose local data centres 75 to 25.

  • Microsoft — is winding down its 2023 deal to rent AI computing from Oracle, because the old Nvidia chips no longer meet its needs. It also dropped Nscale projects in the UK and West Virginia.
  • Oracle — fell below the $116 price at which it sold $10bn of new shares days earlier, and is down 68% from its record.
  • Goldman Sachs — raised its US data centre building forecast anyway, to $125bn for 2028, saying projects are delayed, not cancelled.
  • SemiAnalysis — says 1-year H100 chip rental prices are up 21% since March, and at most 2–3 GW of the ~50 GW due in 2027 is at risk.

Reading between the lines, a delay still costs money. A data centre that opens a year late still pays interest on its loans, and hyperscalers are funding about 36% of 2026 spending with credit. That is why lenders, not only chip makers, are now part of the AI story.

Asia tries to stop the fall

Korea and Taiwan sell the chips and memory the AI build needs, so they fell hardest. Taiwan will cap short selling (selling borrowed shares to bet on a fall) from 9 November and set aside about $15bn to buy shares. Korea suspended its margin-call rule for 3 months.

The price of money: strong US jobs, rising rates, gold level with tech

The US job market is too strong for rate cuts, Americans are borrowing more, and India's central bank has started raising rates.

In short
  • US jobs data is so strong that a US rate rise in December is being discussed again.
  • Americans borrowed at the fastest pace in about 4 years.
  • Gold has done as well as US tech shares since 2023, with far less noise.

The US job market will not cool

Fewer Americans are filing for jobless benefits than at any time in over 50 years, and there are more open jobs than people looking for work. That is good for workers but bad for anyone hoping interest rates will fall.

The last card does not fit the others. Those who lose a job now stay jobless longer. So the market is strong for people already in work but harder for those trying to get back in, which matches stories of AI cutting entry-level and office roles.

The hot payroll report put a December rate rise by the US Federal Reserve (the US central bank) back on the table. The Fed already raised rates slightly in September. JPMorgan's Charles Bristow also expects no spending cuts from the new Congress, only more spending, which keeps government borrowing high.

Americans keep buying and borrowing

US retail investors bought shares on 22 sessions in a row to 3 November. Their buying so far this year is a record $251bn, more than in any full year before.

Berkshire Hathaway also turned buyer. It repurchased $9.6bn of its own shares in the September quarter, after almost 2 years without buybacks, and has been a net buyer of other shares too.

US imports of computers and chips hit a record $86.9bn in September. Apple dropped its goal of holding zero net cash, which gives it room to borrow and spend.

Gold, rates and the rupee

India's Reserve Bank raised the repo rate (the rate at which it lends to banks) to 5.50% on 7 October, its first rise since 2023. Meanwhile gold has kept pace with US tech shares, with far smaller daily swings.

  • Canada — weak jobs data gave the Canadian dollar its worst week since late September.

Oil, ships and the Iran war

Oil fell below $100 on peace hopes, then the US struck Iran again, a sailor was killed at sea and Iran-linked ships circled undersea internet cables.

In short
  • Oil lost most of its war premium on hopes of a US-Iran deal.
  • Then the US hit Iranian military sites, and a tanker crew member was killed in the Gulf.
  • Oil is flowing again, but it spends longer at sea, so cargoes reach refiners late.

The war flared again

Around 1:30 am Tehran time on 7 November, the US struck air defences and military sites on Iran's Qeshm Island. It was the first strike ordered since the US midterm elections. President Trump called it minor and said Iran wants a deal.

  • At sea — a sailor was killed in a suspected missile strike on the empty Greek-managed tanker Velos Sapphire, bound for Bahrain. Several tankers hit sea mines in the Strait of Hormuz.
  • Cables — at least 4 Iran-linked ships, 3 of them US-sanctioned tankers, lingered over undersea internet cables in the Gulf of Oman with their trackers off.
  • Bases — Iran named up to 15 Western bases in 9 countries, including Ramstein in Germany and RAF Fairford in Britain, as targets if they are used to strike it.
  • Staging — Iraq closed its airspace for "military operations" from 7 to 11 November; Israel went to maximum alert; Iran cut internet access and rationed fuel.
  • Talks — Iran is reviewing proposals to curb its nuclear programme, and Qatar's prime minister says a settlement is possible.

Views split on the cables. One view is that Iran keeps the cable threat as a threat only, because cutting them would blind its own sanctioned economy most. The opposite view, from ship-tracking firm Windward, is that the ships' movements fit the pattern of mapping cable routes or laying charges.

A death changes shipping decisions in a way damage does not. Crews can refuse to sail into a war zone under their contracts, and company boards carry a legal duty of care. So a slowdown through Hormuz can happen without another missile being fired.

Oil flows, but arrives late

Middle East exports have recovered, but ships take longer routes and transfer oil at sea, and buyers who avoid the Gulf buy from further away. So more oil sits on tankers instead of in refineries. Russia's fuel exports also hit a 4-month high after it eased diesel export limits.

Reading between the lines, a cheaper barrel does not mean cheap fuel delivered on time. Indian refiners and power users pay for the delay through freight, insurance and stock held at sea, which keeps costs higher than the Brent price suggests.

Gujarat Energy signed a 10-year deal with ADNOC for up to 4 million tonnes of LNG (liquefied natural gas) a year from 2028. It is the first sale from ADNOC's overseas portfolio XRG, so some of the gas can come from outside the Gulf.

Metals: few new mines

The world is finding less copper. New finds have fallen sharply in each period, and the recent ones are smaller and cost more to build. That supports copper prices even when demand slows.

India's market: a big-company rally on foreign money

Foreign funds bought and closed bets on a fall, home funds sold at a record pace, and AI-linked Indian shares fell with their US peers.

In short
  • The Nifty gained about 10% in the 7 sessions before Diwali, its best such run since 1990.
  • Foreign funds bought heavily, while Indian funds sold the most in a week since 2008.
  • Indian shares tied to AI, data centres and power equipment fell, even as the index rose.

Who bought and who sold

On 6 November the Nifty closed at 25,030, back above a level it lost weeks earlier. The Sensex has risen about 13% since 8 October, when the market sat at an 18-month low.

The futures numbers explain much of the jump. Foreign funds had large bets on a fall, and closing them means buying. On 6 November alone they closed over 40,000 short contracts. So part of the rally was foreign funds covering losses, not starting new long-term positions.

One view is that home-fund selling marks a top, since mutual funds are using the rally to raise cash or meet redemptions. The opposite view is that a market where every broker skipped HDFC Bank from its Diwali picks list shows peak gloom, which usually comes before gains, and HDFC Bank was among the best performers that week.

India's economy: strong festive buying, rising prices

Shoppers spent heavily around Dhanteras, but companies are raising prices or shrinking packs, and the government is acting against hoarding.

In short
  • Gold, quick-delivery and online sales jumped around Dhanteras.
  • Electric vehicle sales hit a record, but supply could not keep up.
  • Food and consumer companies say inflation is here to stay, so prices will keep rising.

Dhanteras: more spending, lighter buys

A dip of about ₹3,000 per 10 grams in gold on Dhanteras brought buyers back. People bought lighter, lower-carat pieces, and in smaller towns over 60% of jewellery deals used old gold in exchange.

Prices keep rising

Britannia raised prices by 3.5%, mostly by cutting pack sizes, and its chief Rakshit Hargave says high inflation "is there to stay". Reliance's consumer arm has started selling biscuits in Tamil Nadu after its Campa cola crossed ₹5,250 crore of sales in 6 months.

One view is that biscuits are hard to disrupt because most sell at fixed ₹5 and ₹10 price points. The opposite view is that Campa did the same in colas, so Britannia's defensive tone is a warning in itself.

  • Rice — the Centre doubled its rice sale target to 50 lakh tonnes for FY27 and made traders declare stocks every week, to stop hoarding.
  • Ethanol — Karnataka says some petrol had up to 31% ethanol; oil companies say 893 samples met the 20% limit.
  • Microloans — Satin Creditcare is building a ₹100 crore buffer against El Niño and West Asia risks, though it sees no stress yet.
  • Unilever — its CEO said India will be for Unilever in the next decade what China was in the last one; India gives 16% of its revenue.
  • West Bengal — new Chief Minister Suvendu Adhikari expects ₹1 lakh crore of investment, but the state carries ₹8 lakh crore of debt.

Reading between the lines, the strong demand and the rising prices are linked. Companies can pass on costs because people are still buying. That supports the Reserve Bank's choice to raise rates, and it argues against a quick cut.

Power, factories and defence: India builds the parts

Solar power now floods the grid at midday, the government is paying for storage and battery refineries, and electronics, ships and defence push deeper into making parts.

In short
  • Electricity was free at midday on some days in October, but costly after sunset.
  • The Cabinet approved money for power storage and India's first lithium and nickel refineries.
  • Electronics, shipbuilding and defence firms are moving from assembly to making parts.

Too much power at noon, too little at night

Paying for storage and refineries

The Cabinet approved ₹14,500 crore of viability gap funding (a grant that covers part of a project's cost) for 92 GWh of storage. Battery storage projects must use at least 40% Indian content.

It also approved ₹2,814 crore to pay 30% of the cost of India's first lithium and nickel refineries, the step between the mine and the battery factory.
